How to get experimental values

How to get experimental values

Start with an instrument reading, convert its units, then calculate the requested experimental quantity. A theoretical value comes from a model or reference; compare only after finding the experimental value.

MEASUREMENT 01

Buoyancy: from cylinder and balance readings

Read the cylinder before and after submerging the object. Subtract first; then convert mL to m³. The balance method uses the loss of apparent weight.

Experiment 1 — Buoyancy

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

Archimedes' principle connects an upward buoyant force to the weight of displaced fluid: B = ρf g Vdisplaced. The fluid density ρf is in kg/m³, displaced volume is in m³, and g = 9.80 m/s² in the activity. A fully submerged object displaces its own immersed volume; a floating object displaces only enough fluid to balance its weight.

For the hanging steel mass, compare two independent estimates of buoyant force: B1 = ρf g(Vfinal − Vinitial) and B2 = Wair − Win_fluid. The spring balance reads apparent weight while the object is immersed. Keep the mass off the cylinder bottom; contact with the bottom would add a support force and invalidate the comparison.

For the floating container, equilibrium gives B = W = mg, so ρfluid = m/Vdisplaced. At a fixed object weight, a denser fluid requires a smaller displaced volume. Convert milliliters to cubic meters before using SI density: 1 mL = 10⁻⁶ m³. The activity's prose names different liquids in places; use the actual liquid and its matching theoretical density when comparing results.

Experiment 2 — Linear thermal expansion

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

The image-only pages for Activity 2 show brass-rod measurements and calculations for the coefficient of linear expansion. A rod heated by ΔT changes length by ΔL = α L₀ ΔT, so α = ΔL/(L₀ΔT). Here L₀ is the starting length and α has units of °C⁻¹. A temperature change has the same numerical size in °C and K.

Read the dial gauge scale before substituting: a count of divisions is not automatically a millimeter value. Use the same length unit for ΔL and L₀, and compute ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial. Then compare the experimental α with the theoretical value using absolute relative error divided by the theoretical value.

The scanned pages contain different students' filled readings. Treat them as examples of the calculation flow, not one common data set. The high-value relationship to remember is that, for the same material and temperature rise, a longer initial rod expands more; α is the material property being estimated.

Experiment 3 — Electric charge and the Van de Graaff generator · Notes and solutions

LAB 03 / EXPERIMENT 3

Experiment 3 — Electric charge and the Van de Graaff generator

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

Objects become charged mainly through electron transfer: losing electrons makes an object positive; gaining electrons makes it negative. Total charge is conserved. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract, while a charged object can attract a neutral one by polarizing its charges. This explains why attraction alone does not prove that two objects have opposite net charges.

Rubbing transfers electrons between materials; contact shares charge when objects touch; induction separates charge without initial contact and can leave net charge after a grounding step. Grounding provides a path for electrons to move to or from Earth. The generator's dome creates a strong electric field, which is why nearby materials may react before touching it.

The activity compares foil confetti, stacked tins or plates, a candle flame, tissue strips, a fluorescent lamp, Franklin's bell, and hair. Trace each observation through polarization, contact, repulsion, or grounding rather than memorizing a single label for every demonstration. The procedure says to remove excess dome charge with the discharging sphere after use.

Experiment 4 — Simple circuits, conductors, and insulators

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

A lamp lights only when a closed conducting path connects the cell or supply, the load, and the return path. A switch opens or closes that path. In a single series loop the same current passes through each component, so swapping the order of the cell, switch, and bulb does not by itself change the current.

Conductors allow charge to move relatively easily; insulators strongly impede it. The activity tests several metals and nonmetals by placing each sample into a circuit gap and watching the lamp. The test indicates conductivity in that particular setup; a dark lamp can also result from poor contact, an open switch, or insufficient current.

A resistor limits current. Under otherwise similar conditions, a 100 Ω resistor should permit less current than a 47 Ω resistor, so a series lamp would generally be dimmer. The two bulb types have different ratings, so a brightness comparison also depends on the source voltage and bulb design.

Experiment 5 — Capacitor charging and discharging

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

Capacitance is charge stored per voltage, C = Q/V. In the RC activity, the time constant is τ = RC, with R in ohms and C in farads. Increasing either R or C makes the voltage change more slowly. Convert microfarads before multiplying: 1 µF = 10⁻⁶ F.

The activity measures voltage across the capacitor at multiples of τ, graphs both curves, and compares 470 µF and 100 µF capacitors with 100 kΩ and 10 kΩ resistors. A voltmeter belongs in parallel with the capacitor. At 5τ or 6τ the voltage is very close to its final value, but an ideal exponential curve reaches it only asymptotically.

Experiment 6 — Ohm's law and graph slopes

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

Ohm's law is V = IR. At constant resistance, current is proportional to voltage; at constant voltage, current is inversely proportional to resistance. An ohmic component has approximately constant resistance over the measured operating range, producing an approximately straight V-versus-I graph.

The activity's first graph puts current I on the horizontal axis and voltage V on the vertical axis. Its slope ΔV/ΔI is experimental resistance in ohms. The second graph puts 1/R on the horizontal axis and I on the vertical axis; because I = V(1/R), its slope is experimental supply voltage in volts. Always read axis order before assigning a physical meaning to a slope.

The ammeter is inserted in series and the voltmeter is placed across the component. Convert milliamperes to amperes before computing resistance. Experimental percent error uses |measured − theoretical|/|theoretical| × 100. Measured data in the PDF are examples; a trend can agree with Ohm's law even when its fitted numerical slope has error.

LAB 07 / EXPERIMENT 7

Experiment 7 — Resistivity of a wire

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

The wire model is R = ρL/A, where ρ is material resistivity, L is wire length, and A is cross-sectional area. At the same material and area, R grows with L. At the same material and length, a thicker wire has more area and less resistance. For a round wire A = πd²/4, so doubling diameter would make the ideal resistance one-quarter as large.

The procedure changes one factor at a time: probe spacing for length, brass-wire diameter for area, then material. It obtains resistance from R = V/I and resistivity from ρ = RA/L. Use meters and square meters if reporting ρ in Ω·m; values in the activity table are labeled Ω·cm, so convert consistently before comparing with them.

Resistivity is a material property, whereas resistance also depends on the sample's dimensions. The activity's principle appears to print ρ where it describes the symbol for the ohm; the unit of resistance is Ω, while ρ conventionally denotes resistivity. Read table headings and units carefully when checking a result.

Experiment 8 — Series, parallel, and mixed resistor networks

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

Series resistors share one current path: Rtotal = ΣRi, every resistor carries the same current, and the voltage drops add to the source voltage. Parallel branches share the same two endpoints: every branch has the same voltage, branch currents add, and 1/Rtotal = Σ(1/Ri). The equivalent resistance of a parallel group is below its smallest branch resistance.

For a mixed network, identify actual series and parallel groups from nodes, reduce one group, then work backward. Once total current is known, find the voltage across a series part. That voltage determines the branch voltage of a parallel group, after which branch currents follow from I = V/R. Check both current conservation and voltage sums.

The lab asks for measured and calculated values in several 10 V arrangements. The discussion notes that one set of two-resistor series readings implies unequal currents, which cannot hold for a true series path. Use the circuit rules as a consistency test and recheck labels, meter placement, and readings when data disagree.

Experiment 9 — Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws · Notes and solutions

LAB 09 / EXPERIMENT 9

Experiment 9 — Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) expresses charge conservation at a node: the algebraic sum of currents is zero, or total current entering equals total current leaving. Choose an assumed direction for every branch current. A negative solved value means the real current runs opposite to that assumption.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) expresses energy conservation around a closed loop: the signed sum of potential changes is zero. The activity's sign convention is −IR when traversing a resistor with its current, +IR against the current, +emf through a source from negative to positive, and −emf from positive to negative.

To analyze the lab network, mark current directions and loop directions first, write one node equation and independent loop equations, then solve. Use V = IR to compare predicted resistor voltages with voltmeter measurements. The ammeter is in series with the branch being measured; the voltmeter is in parallel with the component.

Experiment 10 — Plane mirrors · Notes and solutions

LAB 10 / EXPERIMENT 10

Experiment 10 — Plane mirrors

Jump to equipment, questions & solutions ↓

The law of reflection states θincidence = θreflection, with both angles measured from the normal to the mirror, not from the mirror surface. A normal is perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point where the ray meets it.

The activity locates the image by sighting a reflected ray, extending that ray behind the mirror as a dashed line, and finding the apparent intersection with the line perpendicular to the mirror through the object. The image is virtual because the reflected light only appears to come from behind the mirror. In an ideal plane mirror, the perpendicular image distance q equals the object distance p.

Measure p and q perpendicular to the mirror line; measure both ray angles relative to the normal. The activity compares the two distances and two angles by percent difference, using the mean of each pair as denominator. Avoid confusing that symmetric comparison with percent error against a theoretical reference.
